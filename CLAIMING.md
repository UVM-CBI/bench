# Claiming a Task
Stuck on any of this? Ask in `#help`.

## The process

**1. Find one you can actually do.** Read the whole issue — deliverable and
deadline included. If you're not certain you have the skill, that's fine, but say
so when you claim. If the tasks feels harder/longer than its description, contact 
us to raise the concern.

**2. Post in `#open-tasks` in the CBI Bench team.** Reply in that task's thread
with:

- How you'd approach it. Two or three sentences, enough to show you read the
  issue and have a plan.
- When you'd have it done.

That's the whole application. Example:

> I'd design this in OnShape starting from the diagram linked in the issue, and
> check the fit against the printed housing before finalizing. Done by Oct 14.

**3. Wait to be assigned.** You'll get assigned on GitHub and the issue picks up
a `claimed` label. Don't start work before being assigned — occasionally multiple
people might claim the same thing.

**4. Build it.** Ask questions **on the GitHub issue**, not in Teams and not by
email. Everything about a task should be findable in one place, including by
whoever picks it up if you hand it off.

**5. Deliver.** Link your deliverable in an issue comment and add the
`needs-review` label. Files go to OneDrive, OnShape, or wherever the issue
specifies — not committed to this repo.

## What you need

A free GitHub account: <https://github.com/signup>. Use your UVM email and you
can also claim the [student developer pack](https://education.github.com/pack).

## Rules

**Two weeks of no activity releases your claim.** An assigned issue with no activity
for 14 days gets automatically unassigned and returned to the pool. This isn't a
punishment — it's how the board stays honest about what's actually being worked
on.

Avoiding it is easy, e.g. "Still on this, hit a snag with the tolerance, back to it
this weekend".

**One at a time to start.** Close one out before claiming a second. After that,
claim as many as you can handle.

**Handing off is fine and is not a failure.** If you get busy, or it turns out to
be beyond what you can do, say so on the issue. Write down where you got to and
what you learned. Someone else can pick it up from there. That's a genuinely helpful
outcome.

**Ask scope questions before you claim.** "Does this need to be manufacturable or
is a concept model fine?" is a good question. Ask it on the issue.

## What you get

- Public contribution history on your GitHub profile.
- Experience on real-world projects and a low-stakes opportunity to hone existing skills or develop new ones
- Named credit in whatever the work feeds into — paper, poster, larger project.
- A reference from someone who has seen you finish something.

## Ownership of what you make

Most bench tasks carry no strings — you make it, you can show it, and it's
credited to you.

Some tasks are tied to unpublished or patent-pending work. Those say so in the
issue, and there's paperwork to sort out before you start.
